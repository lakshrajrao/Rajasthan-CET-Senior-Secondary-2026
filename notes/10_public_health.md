> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the labels and say the three parts of health from memory. Then point at a disease (Malaria? Diabetes? Cholera?) and say which road it takes — or no road. That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — two patients, two kinds of illness

Imagine a health camp in a Rajasthan village. Two people come in.

**Ramesh, 30, has malaria.** He was bitten by a mosquito last week; now he has fever with chills. His illness *came from outside* — a mosquito carried the parasite into his blood. If the mosquitoes are controlled, Ramesh's kind of illness can be stopped from spreading. His neighbour can catch it too — not from Ramesh directly, but from the same mosquitoes.

**Suresh, 55, has diabetes.** Nobody gave it to him. It built up over years — food habits, weight, family history. You cannot "catch" diabetes from Suresh, and no mosquito carries it. It is treated by changing how he lives and with medicine, not by stopping an infection.

Both men are "sick" — but the two illnesses work in completely different ways. That is why the exam first asks: **is this disease communicable (sankramak) or non-communicable (asankramak)?** The answer decides everything else — prevention, treatment, and which government programme handles it.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why health is more than "no disease"

- **Why does WHO define health as physical + mental + social well-being?** Because a person can have no disease and still not be healthy — a person under constant stress, or cut off from society, is not fully well. The definition is a **convention** (WHO's definition, adopted 1948): there is no deeper "why" beyond the fact that health was deliberately defined broadly so that programmes would cover the whole person, not just germs.
- **Why are diseases grouped by how they spread?** Because prevention follows the route. If a disease spreads through mosquitoes, you fight mosquitoes. If it spreads through dirty water, you clean the water. If it does not spread at all, you change lifestyle. The classification is a **practical tool**, not a law of nature.
- **Why do some diseases need a "vector"?** A vector (like a mosquito) is a carrier — the germ lives part of its life inside the carrier. Malaria's parasite must pass through the Anopheles mosquito; without the mosquito, malaria cannot reach a human. That is why "Malaria — Anopheles" is an exam fact: the vector *is* the route.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — classify any disease in 3 steps

1. **Does it spread from person to person (directly or through a carrier)?** Yes → communicable (sankramak). No → non-communicable (asankramak).
2. **If communicable, which road?** Mosquito/insect (vector-borne) · dirty water/food (water-borne) · air/droplets (air-borne) · blood or body fluids (e.g. AIDS — see §G).
3. **If non-communicable, what drives it?** Usually lifestyle + heredity — diabetes, cancer, heart disease, hypertension. No germ to kill; the treatment is long-term management.

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture health as a **cycle (bicycle) with three wheels** — sharirik, mansik, samajik. A normal cycle has two wheels; this one needs all three. If one wheel is broken, the cycle does not run properly — even if the other two are fine. And picture each disease as a traveller: malaria travels by mosquito, cholera travels by dirty water, TB travels by air, diabetes travels by **no road at all** — it stays where it is.

### F. IMPORTANT FACTS

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|

### G. COMMON CONFUSIONS

| Confusion | Correct difference |
|---|---|
| Communicable vs non-communicable | Communicable **spreads** (TB, malaria). Non-communicable **does not spread** (diabetes, cancer) — it builds up from lifestyle/heredity. |
| Vector-borne vs water-borne | Vector = a **living carrier** (mosquito). Water-borne = **dirty water** itself carries the germ (cholera, typhoid). Malaria is vector-borne, NOT water-borne. |
| Malaria vs Dengue mosquito | Malaria → **Anopheles**. Dengue/Chikungunya → **Aedes**. Two different mosquitoes, two different diseases — never swap them. |
| AIDS is communicable — but by which road? | AIDS spreads through **blood and body fluids**, not through air or water. It is communicable, but NOT air-borne and NOT water-borne. |
| TB vs Flu | Both air-borne, but TB is bacterial and long-term; flu is viral and short-term. Same road, different disease. |

### H. MEMORY TECHNIQUE (one only)

**"V-W-A: Vector, Water, Air"** — the three roads, in the order the exam asks them. Say the road, then one disease for each: **V**ector → Malaria (Anopheles) · **W**ater → Cholera · **A**ir → TB. If a disease takes none of the three roads, it is non-communicable (diabetes).

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — one village, one year, every programme at work

Follow a pregnant woman, Sunita, in a Rajasthan village through one year — and watch the programmes line up around her.

**ASHA didi** (the village health worker) visits her home, checks her health, and tells her to deliver at the hospital, not at home. **Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY, 2005)** supports that institutional delivery, and **Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK)** gives mother and baby free services. The baby is born at the **PHC** (Primary Health Centre); the **ANM** (nurse-midwife) gives the first vaccines — **BCG** at birth, then **OPV** and **DPT** — under **Mission Indradhanush (2014)**, the full-immunisation drive. If the family falls seriously ill, **Ayushman Bharat — PM-JAY (2018)** covers up to **₹5 lakh per year per family** at the hospital; in Rajasthan the state scheme is **Mukhyamantri Ayushman Arogya Yojana** (earlier Chiranjeevi). Meanwhile the village gets clean water under **Jal Jeevan Mission (2019)** and toilets under **Swachh Bharat Mission (2014)** — because dirty water and open defecation are where cholera and diarrhoea begin. The child grows up under **Poshan Abhiyaan (2018)**, the fight against malnutrition, and the village celebrates **Poshan Maah every September**.

One family, one year — and almost every programme in this chapter touches them. That is the point: the programmes are not a list to memorise, they are a chain from village to hospital.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the programmes are built this way

- **Why start with mothers and children?** Because the two exam indicators — IMR (infant deaths) and MMR (maternal deaths) — are measured exactly there. A country that protects birth protects its health numbers. JSY, JSSK, Mission Indradhanush and Poshan Abhiyaan all sit on this logic.
- **Why an ASHA in every village?** Because hospitals are far and doctors are few. The ASHA is the **first link** — she finds the pregnant woman, the unvaccinated child, the TB patient, and connects them to the PHC. Without her, the programmes never reach the village.
- **Why insurance (PM-JAY) instead of only free hospitals?** Because a serious illness can cost a poor family its savings in one week. ₹5 lakh of cover means the family does not have to choose between treatment and ruin. That is also why PM-JAY is described as the world's largest health insurance scheme — it covers the largest number of families.
- **Why does Swachh Bharat sit in a health chapter?** Because toilets and clean water *are* health policy: water-borne diseases (cholera, typhoid) fall when open defecation and dirty water fall. The programme is about sanitation, but its effect is measured in disease.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — answer any programme question in 4 steps

1. **Year** — when did it start? (JSY 2005 · NHM 2013 · Indradhanush/Swachh 2014 · PM-JAY/Poshan 2018 · Jal Jeevan 2019.)
2. **Who benefits?** — pregnant women (JSY/JSSK) · children (Indradhanush) · poor families (PM-JAY) · whole village (Swachh/Jal Jeevan).
3. **How much / what exactly?** — ₹5 lakh (PM-JAY) · free services (JSSK) · full immunisation (Indradhanush).
4. **Which level delivers it?** — ASHA (village) → PHC → CHC → district hospital.

## Chapter 3 — Nutrition, Sanitation & Key Indicators

### A. VISUAL INTRODUCTION — the BMI number line

```
BMI SCALE (kg/m²) — apna vajan napo, phir dekho kahan ho:
◄──────────┼──────────────────┼──────────────────┼──────────►
        < 18.5            18.5–24.9           25–29.9         ≥ 30
       UNDERWEIGHT          NORMAL           OVERWEIGHT        OBESE
   (vajan kam)          (theek)           (vajan zyada)    (motaapa)

IMR aur MMR — "per kitne" se yaad rakho:
   IMR = 1,000 jeevit janm par 1 saal se kam mrityu  (bacche → chhota number)
   MMR = 1,00,000 jeevit janm par matri mrityu      (maa → bada number)
   TFR = ek mahila ke jeevan me ausat bacche (~2.0 = replacement level)
```

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the labels and point at a BMI value (e.g. 17, 22, 27, 32) and say its category. Then say IMR and MMR with their denominators from memory. That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — the anganwadi measuring tape

Every month at the village anganwadi, the worker measures the children: height on the wall, weight on the scale, and a blood check for the mothers. Three measurements, three different problems — and three different words the exam uses.

**A child whose height is too low for his age** is *stunted* — he has been underfed for a long time, and height lost early is hard to regain. **A child whose weight is too low for his height** is *wasted* — he is thin right now, often after illness or a recent food shortage. **A mother whose blood is pale and who tires easily** may have *anaemia* — her blood lacks iron, so it cannot carry enough oxygen.

The anganwadi also records births and deaths — and from those records come the big numbers: **IMR** (how many babies die before age one, per 1,000 live births), **MMR** (how many mothers die, per 1,00,000 live births), and **TFR** (how many children a woman has on average). These three numbers tell the government whether its health programmes are working. That is why they are exam facts: they are the scoreboard of public health.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why these measures exist

- **Why is BMI weight divided by height squared (kg/m²)?** Because weight alone says nothing — a tall person naturally weighs more. Dividing by height² adjusts weight for body size, so one scale works for short and tall people alike. The formula is a **convention** (Quetelet's index, adopted by WHO) — the "why" is comparability, not a law of nature.
- **Why is stunting about height and wasting about weight?** Because they measure different time scales: height grows slowly, so short height means *long-term* undernutrition; weight changes fast, so low weight means *current* shortage or illness. Two words, two time scales — that is why the exam tests them side by side.
- **Why is anaemia linked to iron?** Red blood cells need iron to make haemoglobin, the molecule that carries oxygen. Too little iron → too little haemoglobin → too little oxygen → tiredness and paleness. The chain is cause and effect, not a definition.
- **Why are IMR and MMR measured per 1,000 and per 1,00,000?** Because deaths are rare events. A small village may have zero maternal deaths in a year — so the rate is expressed per 1,00,000 births to make it a usable number. The denominators are **conventions** chosen so the numbers are easy to compare between states and years.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — compute BMI, read an indicator

1. **BMI:** vajan (kg) ÷ [unchai (m) × unchai (m)]. Example: 60 kg, 1.6 m → 60 ÷ 2.56 = **23.4** → Normal (18.5–24.9).
2. **Read the category:** < 18.5 underweight · 18.5–24.9 normal · 25–29.9 overweight · ≥ 30 obese.
3. **Read an indicator:** first find the **denominator** (per 1,000 or per 1,00,000?), then the **group** (infants under 1 year for IMR; mothers for MMR). The denominator is the trap — IMR and MMR are mixed up by students who remember the name but not the "per kitne".
4. **Immunisation order:** BCG at birth (TB) → OPV (polio) → DPT → Measles → Hepatitis B. Birth first, the rest by schedule.

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture the BMI number line as a **road with three signboards**: below 18.5 the board says "underweight — vajan kam", between 18.5 and 24.9 the board says "normal — theek", and at 30 and above the board says "obese — motaapa". And picture IMR and MMR as two buckets: the IMR bucket holds **1,000** births, the MMR bucket holds **1,00,000** — the mother's bucket is a hundred times bigger because maternal deaths are rarer. When in doubt, ask: "bacche ka bucket chhota, maa ka bada?"

|---|---|

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — why this chapter is empty

A student who spends hours scrolling at night sleeps less, wakes tired, and cannot concentrate the next day — that is the everyday picture behind this topic. But the syllabus asks for *health risks and prevention* as a health topic: which risks, how they work, and what prevents them. This repo has **nothing verified** on it: the old notes do not mention social media at all, and none of the 14 bank MCQs test it. Listing risks from everyday opinion ("phones are bad for you") would teach nothing the exam can ask and could state a wrong mechanism. So this chapter stays a **gap marker** until the risks and prevention measures are taken from a standard source.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the gap matters

- **Why is social-media addiction in a Public Health head?** Because it is a new, youth-centred health risk — the syllabus was written in 2026, and this topic exists precisely because screen use is now a health issue. New topics in new heads are prime exam territory.
- **Why is it missing here?** The earlier research phase covered programmes, nutrition and indicators only. The audit register (§G3) records social-media addiction as an open gap (grep confirms zero coverage in notes and bank).
- **Why not fill it from opinion?** Because "too much screen time is bad" is not a testable fact. The exam needs the risks *as stated by standard sources* (sleep, attention, mental health, physical inactivity) and the prevention measures *as recommended* — both must be sourced.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to close this gap (steps, not content)

1. **Research from a standard source** — e.g. NCERT *Health and Physical Education*; WHO guidance on screen time and adolescent health; UNICEF/WHO material on digital well-being.

3. **Write the chapter in the A–K format** (with a risks table, prevention table, confusions, recall questions).
4. **Add bank questions** only after the facts are verified, and mark their source.
