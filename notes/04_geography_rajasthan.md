### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — one state, four different worlds

Drive across Rajasthan and the land changes completely four times. Start in **Jaisalmer** in the west: sand dunes, almost no rain, villages far apart — the Thar desert, about 60% of the state. Drive east and a long line of old hills rises across your path — the **Aravalli**, running from the north-east to the south-west, cutting the state in two. Cross it and the land turns green and flat: the **Eastern Plain**, where the Banas and Chambal flow and wheat and mustard grow. Keep going south-east and the land rises again into a rocky plateau — **Hadoti** (Kota, Bundi, Jhalawar, Baran), part of the Malwa plateau, with black soil and the Chambal's dams. One state, four landscapes — and almost every exam question about Rajasthan's geography starts from this four-part map.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the four divisions exist

- **Why is the west a desert?** The standard textbook explanation: the Aravalli hills run **parallel** to the south-west monsoon winds coming from the Arabian Sea, so the winds slide along the range instead of being forced up over it — and air that is not forced up does not cool and drop rain. The west is also far from the sea and sits in the dry subtropical belt. Result: Barmer gets only ~10 cm of rain a year, while Jhalawar in the east gets ~100 cm.
- **Why is the Aravalli so low and old?** It is one of the world's **oldest** mountain ranges (formed in the Precambrian era, hundreds of millions of years ago). It has been eroding ever since, so its peaks are low and rounded — the highest, Guru Shikhar, is only 1,722 m.
- **Why is the east fertile and the south-east rocky?** The eastern plain is built by river alluvium (Banas, Chambal) — soft, flat, fertile. Hadoti is a lava plateau (part of the Malwa plateau), so its surface is rocky and its soil is the black cotton soil formed from weathered lava.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to read any physical-division question

1. **Find the place on the mental map** (west / Aravalli / east / south-east).
2. **Ask which division it belongs to** — the district name usually gives it away (Jaisalmer = Thar; Kota = Hadoti; Tonk = eastern plain).
3. **Attach the feature** — desert ↔ sand and low rain; Aravalli ↔ hills and the watershed; plain ↔ rivers and farming; Hadoti ↔ plateau and black soil.
4. **Check the superlative** — largest area, highest peak, oldest range — these are the exam's favourite traps.

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture Rajasthan as a **tilted plate**: high and dry in the west (Thar), a long **spine** running diagonally across the middle (the Aravalli), a flat green **tray** in the east (the plain), and a raised rocky **corner** in the south-east (Hadoti). The spine divides the plate: water on its west side runs to the Rann of Kutch; water on its east side runs to the Yamuna and the Ganga.

## Chapter 2 — Climate & Soils

### A. VISUAL INTRODUCTION — the rainfall gradient


### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — the same monsoon, ten times less rain

The south-west monsoon reaches Rajasthan every year — but it does not treat the state equally. In **Barmer**, in the far west, a farmer may see only ~10 cm of rain in a whole year; his crops depend on wells and the Indira Gandhi Canal. Travel 500 km east to **Jhalawar**, and the same monsoon gives ~100 cm — ten times more — enough for soyabean and oranges. The soil changes with the rain: sand in the west, red-yellow soil in the middle, rich alluvium in the eastern plain, and black cotton soil in Hadoti. Climate and soil are not two topics — they are one story told twice: **water decides what the land can grow.**

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why rain increases from west to east

- **Why so little rain in the west?** The standard textbook explanation: the Aravalli runs **parallel** to the monsoon winds from the Arabian Sea, so the winds are not forced up and do not drop their moisture. The west is also far inland and dry, so what little moisture arrives is quickly used up.
- **Why more rain in the east?** The eastern districts are closer to the Bay of Bengal branch of the monsoon, which travels up the Ganga plain and still carries moisture when it reaches eastern Rajasthan. The Aravalli also catches some rain on its eastern side.
- **Why black soil in Hadoti and not elsewhere?** Black soil (regur) forms from the weathering of lava rock — and Hadoti is part of the Malwa plateau, built of Deccan lava. It holds moisture well, which is why it suits cotton.
- **Why red-yellow soil in the middle?** It forms from the weathering of the old crystalline and metamorphic rocks of the Aravalli region; iron oxide gives it the red-yellow colour. It is less fertile than alluvium and needs fertiliser and irrigation.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to answer any climate/soil question

1. **Place the district on the west→east line** (Barmer = far west; Jhalawar = far east).
2. **Read the rainfall off the gradient** — west low, east high.
3. **Match the soil to the landform** — desert ↔ sand; Aravalli region ↔ red-yellow; river plain ↔ alluvial; Hadoti plateau ↔ black.
4. **Attach the crop** — black soil ↔ cotton; alluvial ↔ wheat, mustard; desert ↔ bajra (with irrigation).

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

The ground under the tap changes colour as it gets wetter: pale sand → red-yellow → dark alluvium → black cotton soil. **More water = darker, richer soil.**

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — a tree that saves a desert village

In a Barmer village, the most valuable tree is not a tall timber tree — it is the **Khejri**. Its deep roots find water far below the sand; its leaves feed goats in the dry months; its pods feed people; its shade cools the courtyard. That is why it is the state tree — it is the tree the desert lives on. The **Rohida**, with its bright orange-yellow flowers, blooms in the dry heat when little else does — the state flower. And in the open grasslands, the **Godawan** (Great Indian Bustard), a heavy bird that barely flies, walks the ground — the state bird, and one of India's most endangered birds. Vegetation in Rajasthan is not about dense forests: with only ~9.6% forest cover, it is about **dry scrub, thorny trees and grassland** that survive on very little water.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the vegetation looks like this

- **Why so little forest (~9.6%)?** Most of the state is arid or semi-arid: low rainfall (10–100 cm), sandy or rocky soil, and high evaporation. Dense forest needs steady water; Rajasthan's water is scarce and seasonal.
- **Why thorny trees like Khejri?** Thorns, small leaves and deep roots are desert survival tools: they cut water loss and reach deep groundwater. The Khejri's roots can go tens of metres down.
- **Why is the Godawan endangered?** It lives on open grassland and nests on the ground — so it loses habitat to farming and power lines, and its eggs and chicks are easy prey. (Conservation detail is in Chapter 9.)
- **Why a camel as state animal (2014)?** The camel is the desert's working animal — transport, milk and farming in the Thar — and its numbers were falling, so the state gave it the symbolic status to protect it. The Chinkara was already treated as the state animal in many sources, which is why both names appear — see §G.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to answer any vegetation/symbol question

1. **Ask "tree, flower, bird or animal?"** — the exam almost always asks one category at a time.
2. **Match the category to the list** — Khejri = tree; Rohida = flower; Godawan = bird; Chinkara/Camel = animal (read the options — see §G).
3. **Attach the reason** — Khejri = desert survival; Godawan = endangered grassland bird.
4. **Check the number** — forest cover ~9.6% (ISFR) is the only figure here.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — five rivers, two seas

Stand on the Aravalli near Udaipur and pour a bucket of water on the ridge. Half of it runs east — down the **Chambal** past Kota, joined by the **Banas** from Rajsamand, on toward the Yamuna and the Ganga and finally the Bay of Bengal. The other half runs west and south — the **Luni** winding through the desert until it disappears into the salt flats of the Rann of Kutch, never reaching the sea; the **Mahi** and **Sabarmati** running south into Gujarat toward the Gulf of Khambhat. The Aravalli is not just a line of hills — it is the **watershed** that decides which sea every drop of Rajasthan's rain is heading for. And one river breaks the pattern in a useful way: the **Banas** never leaves Rajasthan at all — it is the longest river that flows *only* inside the state, which is why the exam loves it.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why each river flows the way it does

- **Why do rivers flow east or west?** Water follows the **slope (gradient)**. The land east of the Aravalli slopes down toward the Ganga plain (east), so the Chambal and Banas flow east/north-east. The land west of the Aravalli slopes down toward the Rann of Kutch (south-west), so the Luni flows south-west.
- **Why is the Chambal perennial (year-round) while the Luni is not?** The Chambal rises in the wetter Malwa plateau and is fed by tributaries and dams; the Luni rises in the dry Aravalli near Ajmer and flows through the desert, where evaporation is high and rain is low — so it dries up in stretches and is saline (it picks up salts from the desert soil).
- **Why is the Luni saline?** It flows through saline desert soils and has **no outlet to the sea** — its water evaporates in the Rann, leaving the salts behind. (Same reason as Sambhar lake — see Chapter 5.)
- **Why is the Banas called "Van ki Asha" (Hope of the Forest)?** It waters the forested and farming country of south-eastern Rajasthan (Rajsamand, Udaipur, Chittorgarh, Tonk) — the exam only needs the name and the fact that it is Rajasthan's longest **intra-state** river.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to answer any river question

1. **Find the river's side of the Aravalli** — east side → Yamuna/Ganga system; west side → Rann/sea.
2. **Name its origin and its end** — the exam asks "origin → terminus" more than anything else.
3. **Attach its dam or its nickname** — Chambal ↔ 3 dams; Banas ↔ "Van ki Asha"; Luni ↔ Rann of Kutch.
4. **Check the superlative** — longest *in Rajasthan only* = Banas; longest *perennial* river of Rajasthan = Chambal.

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture the Aravalli as a **roof ridge**: rain falling on the east slope runs down one side of the roof (Chambal, Banas → Yamuna), rain on the west slope runs down the other side (Luni → Rann). The Banas is the stream that runs **along the roof** without ever leaving the house — it stays inside Rajasthan from Rajsamand to Tonk.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — a lake that makes salt, and a canal that makes a desert farm

Two kinds of water tell Rajasthan's story. In the east, **Udaipur** is the "City of Lakes" — Pichola, Fatehsagar, Udaisagar — built by kings in the hills, full of fresh water. In the middle of the state sits **Sambhar**, a shallow lake so salty that it has produced salt for centuries — India's largest saline lake. And in the south, **Jaisamand** (Dhebar) is a vast artificial lake built by damming a river. But the biggest story is not a lake at all: it is the **Indira Gandhi Canal**, which carries water from the Harike Barrage in Punjab hundreds of kilometres into the Thar desert, turning sand into farmland. Without stored and carried water, most of Rajasthan could not farm at all.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the lakes are where they are

- **Why is Sambhar saline?** Sambhar has **no outlet**: rivers flow in, but no river flows out. In the hot, dry climate the water evaporates, and the salts it carried stay behind and build up year after year. (Same reason the Luni is saline — see Chapter 4.)
- **Why are Udaipur's lakes fresh?** They sit in the wetter Aravalli hills, are fed by rain and streams, and have outlets — so salts are flushed out instead of accumulating.
- **Why are Jaisamand and Rajsamand artificial?** They were created by building dams across rivers (Jaisamand by damming the Gomti river) to store monsoon water for the dry months. Jaisamand is one of the largest artificial lakes in Asia — though sources differ on whether it is the largest or the second largest (see §G).
- **Why does the IGNP start at Harike Barrage in Punjab?** The canal takes Sutlej–Beas water from the Harike Barrage and carries it south-west into Rajasthan's desert districts. Without it, the Thar's farms would depend only on ~10 cm of rain.
- **Why does Bisalpur matter to Jaipur?** Bisalpur dam (Tonk, on the Banas) stores water that is supplied to Jaipur city — the capital's drinking water depends on it.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how water reaches a Rajasthani field

1. **Monsoon rain** falls (mostly June–September, mostly in the east).
2. **Rivers and dams store it** — Chambal's three dams, Bisalpur on the Banas, Mahi Bajaj Sagar on the Mahi.
3. **Canals carry it** — the IGNP carries Punjab's river water into the Thar; smaller canals serve the east.
4. **Lakes hold the rest** — natural lakes (Pushkar, Nakki, Udaipur's lakes) and artificial lakes (Jaisamand, Rajsamand).
5. **Salt lakes are harvested, not drunk** — Sambhar's water is evaporated for salt, not used for farming.

Now picture a **bucket with a hole at the bottom** (Udaipur's lakes): water flows through, so it stays fresh. And picture a **long pipe** (IGNP) carrying water from Punjab into the desert — the pipe is what makes the Thar farm.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — the desert that sells stone, metal and sunlight

Rajasthan looks dry, but under its ground and above it are three fortunes. Under the ground near Udaipur, the **Zawar** mines have yielded zinc and lead since ancient times — the largest zinc-lead area in India. Near Makrana, white **marble** has been cut for centuries (the same marble that built the Taj Mahal). In Barmer, under the desert sand, lies the **Mangala oil field** — crude oil in the Thar. And above the ground, the desert's harshest feature — its fierce, cloudless sun — has become its newest fortune: **Bhadla Solar Park** near Jodhpur, one of the largest solar parks in the world, in a state that is now No.1 in India for solar power. The desert sells what it has: stone, metal, oil — and sunlight.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why these minerals are here

- **Why zinc-lead at Zawar?** The Aravalli region is old, folded rock — the kind of geology where metal ores (zinc, lead, copper) form in veins. Zawar's deposits have been worked since ancient times because the ore is rich and close to the surface.
- **Why marble at Makrana and Rajsamand?** Marble is metamorphosed limestone, and the Aravalli region has thick limestone beds that were transformed by heat and pressure long ago. Makrana's white marble is famous for its purity.
- **Why oil in Barmer and gas in Jaisalmer?** The Barmer–Jaisalmer basin is a rift basin filled with sedimentary rock — the kind of rock where oil and gas collect. The desert sand on top hides it; drilling found it.
- **Why is solar No.1 here?** The Thar has what solar panels need most: **high solar irradiance, vast barren land, clear skies and very little rain**. A solar park needs empty land and sun — the desert has both in abundance.
- **Why the renewable figures need a check:** the old notes quote November 2026 figures (total renewable ~51.6 GW, solar ~45 GW, RE-SAF award Nov 2026) from ET Rajasthan. These figures were **not independently verified** in the repo audit — see §G.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to answer any mineral/energy question

1. **Ask "mineral or energy?"** — minerals go to a district; energy goes to a place and a rank.
2. **Match mineral ↔ district** — Zawar ↔ zinc-lead; Makrana ↔ marble; Barmer ↔ oil.
3. **Match energy ↔ place** — Bhadla ↔ solar (Jodhpur); Mangala ↔ oil (Barmer).
4. **Check the superlative** — "India's largest" (Zawar zinc-lead), "No.1 in solar" (Rajasthan), "among the world's largest" (Bhadla).

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — farming where it barely rains

A farmer in Barmer gets ~10 cm of rain a year. He cannot grow rice — rice needs standing water. He grows **bajra**, a coarse grain that survives on very little water, and it is the crop Rajasthan grows more of than any other state in India. In the east, where the rain is better and the canals reach, the winter fields turn yellow with **sarso** (mustard) — Rajasthan is No.1 in mustard too, and mustard oil is in every Rajasthani kitchen. In the dry west, farmers also grow **jeera, dhaniya and isabgol** — small, high-value crops that need little water and sell for a good price. Rajasthan's farming is not about growing everything; it is about growing **what the dry land allows, and growing it better than anyone else.**

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why these crops, and why No.1

- **Why bajra in the dry west?** Bajra (pearl millet) is a hardy coarse grain: it grows in sandy soil with very little water and high heat. It is the natural crop of a desert-edge state — which is why Rajasthan leads India in its production.
- **Why mustard in the east?** Sarso is a winter (rabi) oilseed that needs cool weather and moderate water — the eastern plain, with better rain and canal irrigation, suits it. Rajasthan's large sown area makes it No.1.
- **Why jeera, dhaniya and isabgol in the west?** These are low-water, high-value crops: they need little irrigation, tolerate dry heat, and earn more per hectare than grain — a rational choice where water is the scarcest input.
- **Why does irrigation decide the crop map?** Where the IGNP canal reaches, wheat and other water-needing crops appear; where it does not, only bajra, jowar and spices survive. Water, not soil, is the binding constraint.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to answer any crop question

1. **Ask "which crop?"** — the exam asks No.1 crops most often.
2. **Match crop ↔ water need** — low water = bajra, jowar, jeera; more water = wheat, mustard.
3. **Attach the rank** — Bajra No.1, Sarso No.1 (both Rajasthan).
4. **Check the trap** — wheat and rice are **not** Rajasthan's No.1 crops; do not pick them.

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the district column and say each park's district from memory. Then cover the "famous for" column and say what each park is famous for. That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — from hunting grounds to protected parks

Most of Rajasthan's famous parks began as the hunting grounds of kings. **Ranthambore** was the hunting forest of the Jaipur maharajas; **Sariska** belonged to the Alwar rulers; **Keoladeo** was the Bharatpur maharajas' duck-shooting wetland. After independence, the same forests were given a new job: protection. Ranthambore and Sariska became tiger country under Project Tiger; Keoladeo became a bird paradise and a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1982); the **Desert National Park** near Jaisalmer protects the desert itself — including the Godawan, the state bird, which lives on its open ground. The story of these parks is the story of a change in purpose: from shooting animals to saving them.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why these parks are where they are

- **Why tigers in Ranthambore and Sariska?** Both are Aravalli hill forests with water, prey (deer, sambar) and cover — the three things a tiger needs. Ranthambore's lakes and Sariska's valleys hold prey through the dry months.
- **Why birds in Keoladeo?** Keoladeo is a shallow wetland fed by monsoon water. Shallow water + fish + insects = food for thousands of migratory birds. That is why it became a UNESCO site in 1982.
- **Why a Desert National Park?** The Thar's wildlife (Godawan, desert fox, chinkara) needs open desert, not forest. A normal forest park would not protect it — so a park was made *of* the desert itself, near Jaisalmer.
- **Why was the Godawan's home made a park?** The Great Indian Bustard nests on open ground and is one of India's most endangered birds; the Desert National Park protects its breeding ground. (Detail is in Chapter 3.)

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to answer any park question

1. **Ask "park or reserve?"** — the exam uses the official name: Sariska is a *Tiger Reserve*; the others are *National Parks*.
2. **Match park ↔ district** — Ranthambore ↔ Sawai Madhopur; Sariska ↔ Alwar; Keoladeo ↔ Bharatpur; Desert NP ↔ Jaisalmer; Mukundra Hills ↔ Kota.
3. **Match park ↔ famous for** — tiger, tiger, birds, desert wildlife.
4. **Check the UNESCO tag** — only **Keoladeo (1982)** carries it among these five.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — a big state with thinly spread people

Rajasthan is India's largest state by area — but only about 6.85 crore people lived there in 2011, spread over 3,42,239 sq km. That works out to **200 people per sq km**, while India as a whole had 382. Why so thin? Because 60% of the state is desert, where few people can live — the population crowds into the watered east and the cities, and thins out toward the west. The **sex ratio (928)** and **literacy (66.1%)** are the other two numbers the exam asks about: both were below the national average in 2011, which is why they are remembered as "low" figures. ### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the numbers are what they are

- **Why is the density low (200 vs India's 382)?** Density = population ÷ area. Rajasthan has a huge area (the denominator) and a desert covering ~60% of it, where farming and water are scarce — so people cluster in the east and in towns, and the west stays empty. A large area with a dry west mathematically gives a low average density.
- **Why is the sex ratio 928?** The census counted 928 females per 1,000 males in 2011 — below the national figure (943). The reasons are social and demographic (and debated among researchers); the exam only needs the number, not a causal theory — so this chapter states the figure without inventing a single cause.
- **Why is literacy 66.1%?** In 2011, about two-thirds of the population aged 7+ could read and write — below the national average (73% in 2011). Again, the exam needs the number; the causes (school access in desert districts, female literacy gaps) are real but the repo does not have verified district-level figures, so they are not stated here.

1. **Name the four figures** — population, density, sex ratio, literacy.

3. **Attach the year** — all four figures are **Census 2011**. Never quote them as current figures.
4. **Check the unit** — density is "per sq km"; sex ratio is "females per 1,000 males"; literacy is a percentage.
