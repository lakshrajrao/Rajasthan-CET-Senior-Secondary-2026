### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — a year of launches, announcements and records

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why these events are asked

- **Why is a satellite launch an exam question?** Because it tests three things at once: the satellite's name (NVS-03), the system it serves (NavIC — India's own navigation system), and the vehicle and place (GSLV-F18, Sriharikota). One event, four facts — that is why launches are favourite CET questions.
- **Why does the Census matter for Current Affairs?** Because the exam's population questions (density, sex ratio, literacy) all use 2011 data. If you know the 2021 Census was deferred and 2027 was announced, you will not be confused by a question that mentions a "new Census".

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the state chapter is asked this way

Budget questions are almost always "which figure is correct" — so the numbers must be exact.

The exam exploits this clustering: "which event happened on 6 November?" has three plausible answers, and only the date separates them.

They are taught as "as per the bank/notes — verify before the exam", never as settled fact.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — six Nobels, one Indian medal, one national award

The Nobel season of 2026 ran from **5 to 12 October**. Medicine went to **Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel** for **optogenetics** — controlling brain cells with light. Physics went to **Francis Halzen** for the **IceCube Neutrino Observatory** — a giant detector buried in Antarctic ice that catches neutrinos. Chemistry went to **Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai** for **asymmetric synthesis** — making one mirror-image of a molecule instead of both. Literature went to Canadian poet **Anne Carson**. Peace went to **Navi Pillay** of South Africa, the former UN Human Rights chief. Economics went to **Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford** for work on macroeconomic stabilisation. Ten days later, on **22 October**, Indian physicist **Ashoke Sen** (ICTS-TIFR, Bengaluru) became the **first Indian** to win the **Oskar Klein Medal** for theoretical physics. And in India, two national moments bookended the season: on **1 August**, NSA **Ajit Doval** received the **Lokmanya Tilak National Award** in Pune from Amit Shah; on **18 August**, the **National Sports Awards 2025** were announced — **17 Arjuna awardees** (verify before exam) and **no Khel Ratna**.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why awards are the most predictable CA questions

- **Why are the Nobels asked every year?** Because they are six fixed categories with six fixed announcement days — the exam can ask "who won Physics?" or "optogenetics belongs to which prize?" with a single correct answer. The *field* is the real test: "IceCube" means Physics, "optogenetics" means Medicine, "asymmetric synthesis" means Chemistry.
- **Why does "first Indian" matter?** The exam loves firsts. Ashoke Sen being the first Indian Oskar Klein medallist is exactly the kind of fact that becomes a one-mark question — and the trap is to confuse it with a Nobel.
- **Why are the National Sports Awards in this chapter, not Sports?** Because the question is about the *award* (who, how many Arjuna), not about a match result. Keep awards and results in different mental boxes.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — how to answer any award question

1. **Award → person** (Nobel Physics → Halzen).
2. **Person → field/reason** (Halzen → IceCube neutrinos).
3. **Field → category** (IceCube → Physics, not Chemistry).
4. **Check the country** — Carson (Canada), Pillay (South Africa), Sen (India, first Indian).

The picture tells you the field; the field tells you the prize.

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the dates and say each day from its month. Days are fixed — they are the easiest marks in Current Affairs, because they never change.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — why days are asked every year

Every year the CET asks at least one "which day is celebrated on which date" question, because the answer never changes. **Rajasthan Diwas (30 March)** marks the birth of Greater Rajasthan in 1949 (Chapter: History sample — 30 March 1949, the four big states joined). **National Sports Day (29 August)** is Dhyan Chand's birthday. **Constitution Day (26 November)** marks the day the Constitution was adopted in 1949 — not to be confused with **Republic Day (26 January)**, when it came into force. **Hindi Diwas (14 September)** marks Hindi's adoption as an official language. The rest — Environment Day (5 June), Yoga Day (21 June), Teachers' Day (5 September), World Health Day (7 April), Earth Day (22 April), Independence Day (15 August) — are fixed international or national observances. Learn them as a calendar, not as a list.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why fixed dates are free marks

- **Why are days asked at all?** Because they test whether the student knows the calendar of national life. A day question has exactly one correct date — no interpretation, no figure to verify. That makes it the cheapest mark in the paper.
- **Why do students still get them wrong?** Because similar days sit close together: 26 January vs 26 November (both "26th", both about the Constitution); 5 September vs 14 September (both September, both about language/learning); 5 June vs 22 April (both about the environment). The confusions in §G exist precisely because the dates are close.
- **Why does the "Kya? Kab? Kaun/Kitna?" method work?** Because every CA question — not just days — is answered by those three points: *what* happened, *when*, *who/how much*. If you can answer all three for an event, you can answer any MCQ about it.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — the revision method, step by step

1. **For every event, write three points** — Kya? (what) Kab? (when — date/month) Kaun/Kitna? (who/how much).
2. **Test yourself from the three points** — cover the answers, say them aloud, then check. That is retrieval practice (§J of every chapter).
3. **Revisit on the schedule** — today → same day evening → next day (redraw the timeline) → after 3 days (mixed) → after 7 days (timed test).
4. **In the last week before the exam, add the update** — events from 12 Nov – 1 Dec 2026 (register §G16) go into this chapter's timeline first, because they are the freshest and therefore the most likely to be asked.
