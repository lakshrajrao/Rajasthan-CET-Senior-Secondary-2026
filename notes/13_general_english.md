## Chapter 1 — Tenses & Sequence of Tenses

### A. VISUAL INTRODUCTION — the 12-tense grid

```
                    SIMPLE            CONTINUOUS              PERFECT               PERFECT CONTINUOUS
PRESENT    │  She reads.       │  She is reading.      │  She has read.        │  She has been reading.
           │  (roz karti hai)  │  (abhi kar rahi hai)  │  (kar chuki hai)      │  (subah se kar rahi hai)
PAST       │  She read.        │  She was reading.     │  She had read.        │  She had been reading.
           │  (kal kiya)       │  (kar rahi thi)       │  (kar chuki thi)      │  (der tak kar rahi thi)
FUTURE     │  She will read.   │  She will be reading. │  She will have read.  │  She will have been reading.
           │  (kal karegi)     │  (kar rahi hogi)      │  (kar chuki hogi)     │  (der tak kar rahi hogi)
  3 TIMES (Present / Past / Future)  ×  4 ASPECTS (Simple / Continuous / Perfect / Perfect Continuous)  =  12 TENSES
```

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover one row, say the three forms from the time word. Cover one column, say the four forms from the aspect. That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — one day, twelve ways to say it

Take one student, Meena, and one action — reading a book. **"She reads every day"** is her habit (Present Simple). **"She is reading now"** is what she is doing at this moment (Present Continuous). **"She has read the book"** — the book is finished (Present Perfect). **"She has been reading since morning"** — she started in the morning and is still reading (Present Perfect Continuous — this is bank question 001). Yesterday the same four shapes shift back: **"She read"**, **"She was reading"**, **"She had read"**, **"She had been reading"**. Tomorrow they shift forward with *will*. The action never changes — only the **time** (when) and the **aspect** (finished? ongoing?) change. Every tense question in the exam is just: *which time, which aspect?*

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why English needs 12 tenses

- **Why three times?** English marks *when* something happens on the verb itself — past, present or future. Hindi does the same (padhta hai / padha / padhega), so the idea is familiar; only the forms differ.
- **Why four aspects?** Time alone is not enough. "She read" (finished, simple) and "She was reading" (in progress, continuous) are both past, but they say different things. Aspect answers: *was it a habit, in progress, completed, or continuing up to now?* That is why 3 × 4 = 12.
- **Why does "since" go with Perfect Continuous?** *Since* names the starting point of an action that is still going on — "has been reading **since morning**". A starting point + still going on = Perfect Continuous. (This is a convention of English usage, not a law of nature.)

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — identify any tense in 4 steps

1. **Find the helping verb** — is / am / are / was / were / has / have / had / will / will have / has been.
2. **Find the main verb's form** — V1 (read), V-ing (reading), V3 (read/eaten), V1 after *will*.
3. **Match the pair to the grid** — e.g. *has been* + V-ing = Present Perfect Continuous; *was* + V-ing = Past Continuous.
4. **Check the time word** — *since morning / yesterday / tomorrow / every day* confirms the time.

**Sequence of tenses (the rule that links tenses together):** when the main clause is in the **past**, the subordinate clause usually shifts one step back — Present Simple → Past Simple, Present Perfect → Past Perfect. ("He said he **was** busy" — not "is busy" — see Chapter 3.)

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture a **camera**: Simple = a **photo** (one moment, finished); Continuous = a **video** (action in progress); Perfect = a **finished album** (completed before now); Perfect Continuous = a **video that started earlier and is still recording**. *Has been reading since morning* = a video that started in the morning and is still recording now.

### F. IMPORTANT FACTS

| Tense | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | V1 / V1 + s/es | She **reads** every day. |
| Present Continuous | is/am/are + V-ing | She **is reading** now. |
| Present Perfect | has/have + V3 | She **has read** the book. |
| Present Perfect Continuous | has/have been + V-ing | She **has been reading since morning**. |
| Past Simple | V2 | He **went** yesterday. |
| Past Continuous | was/were + V-ing | She **was reading** at 8 pm. |
| Past Perfect | had + V3 | She **had read** it before class. |
| Past Perfect Continuous | had been + V-ing | She **had been reading** for two hours. |
| Future Simple | will + V1 | She **will read** tomorrow. |
| Future Continuous | will be + V-ing | She **will be reading** at 10 am. |
| Future Perfect | will have + V3 | She **will have read** it by Sunday. |
| Future Perfect Continuous | will have been + V-ing | She **will have been reading** for three hours. |

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| **8 Parts of Speech** | Noun, Pronoun, Verb, Adjective, Adverb, Preposition, Conjunction, Interjection |
| **Subject–Verb Agreement** | "Each of the students **is** present" — *Each* is singular, so the verb is singular. |
| **Subject–Verb Agreement** | "Neither Ram nor Shyam **is** here" — *Neither* is singular. |

### G. COMMON CONFUSIONS

| Confusion | Correct difference |
|---|---|
| Present Perfect vs Present Perfect Continuous | *Has read* = finished ("She has read the book"). *Has been reading* = started earlier, still going on ("She has been reading since morning"). |
| Simple Past vs Present Perfect | *Went yesterday* = finished past with a past time word. *Has gone* = past action with present relevance — never use it with *yesterday*. |
| "Each/Every/Neither" with a plural noun | The subject is *Each/Every/Neither* (singular), not the noun after it: "Each of the students **is** present" — *students* is plural, but the verb follows *Each*. |
| *Since* with Simple Past | "She has been ill **since** Monday" — *since* needs a perfect tense, not Simple Past ("She was ill since Monday" is wrong). |
| *Has been* + V1 | Wrong — Perfect Continuous always needs V-ing: "has been **reading**", never "has been **read**". |

## Chapter 2 — Active & Passive Voice

### A. VISUAL INTRODUCTION — the same sentence, two cameras

```
ACTIVE (doer first):     Ram  ──writes──▶  a letter        (Ram = subject, letter = object)
                         S        V           O

PASSIVE (receiver first): A letter  ──is written──▶  by Ram   (letter = subject, Ram = optional)
                          O          be + V3        by + S

FORMULA:  Object  +  be (tense ke hisab se)  +  V3  (+ by + subject)
```

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the passive side and convert three active sentences yourself. Then cover the active side and convert back. That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — who does it, and who receives it

"**Ram writes a letter.**" The camera is on Ram — he is doing the writing. Now point the camera at the letter instead: "**A letter is written by Ram.**" Same event, same meaning — but now the letter is the subject, and Ram is pushed to the end, after *by*. Sometimes Ram disappears altogether: "**A letter was written.**" Nobody says who wrote it — and that is exactly when the passive is useful: when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or obvious. A notice does not say "The clerk issued this order" — it says "**This order is issued**". The exam asks you to flip the camera from one side to the other, keeping the **tense the same**.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the passive exists

- **Why change the subject?** Because the receiver of the action matters more than the doer — in reports, notices, news and science, the *thing done* is the news, not the person who did it. (This is a convention of English style, not a law of nature.)
- **Why does the tense stay the same?** The passive changes the *voice* (who is the subject), not the *time*. "Ram writes" (present) becomes "A letter **is** written" (present); "She ate" (past) becomes "An apple **was** eaten" (past). Change the tense and you have changed the meaning.
- **Why is the verb always V3?** Because *be* + past participle is the fixed shape of the passive in English — *is written, was eaten, has been written*. There is no passive without V3.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — convert any active sentence in 5 steps

1. **Find the object** of the active sentence — it becomes the new subject. ("a letter", "an apple")
2. **Choose the *be* form that matches the tense** — present: is/am/are; past: was/were; perfect: has/have been; future: will be.
3. **Add V3** (past participle) of the main verb — *written, eaten, done*.
4. **Add "by + doer" if the doer matters** — often it can be dropped ("A letter was written" is complete).
5. **Check the tense** — the passive sentence must be in the same tense as the active one.

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture a **stage**: in the active voice, the **actor** stands in the spotlight (Ram writes). In the passive voice, the spotlight moves to the **prop** (the letter is written) and the actor steps into the shadows — sometimes off the stage entirely. Passive = spotlight on the receiver.

### F. IMPORTANT FACTS

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | Ram **writes** a letter. | A letter **is written** by Ram. |
| Past Simple | She **ate** an apple. | An apple **was eaten** by her. |
| Present Perfect | Ram **has written** a letter. | A letter **has been written** by Ram. |
| Past Perfect | She **had eaten** an apple. | An apple **had been eaten** by her. |
| Future Simple | Ram **will write** a letter. | A letter **will be written** by Ram. |
| Present Continuous | Ram **is writing** a letter. | A letter **is being written** by Ram. |
| Modal (can/must) | Ram **can write** a letter. | A letter **can be written** by Ram. |

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Passive = be + V3, hamesha | "is **written**", "was **eaten**" — V3 ke bina passive nahi banta. |
| Tense badalta nahi | Present active → present passive; past active → past passive. |
| *by*-phrase optional | "A letter was written" — poora vakya hai, doer batana zaroori nahi. |

### G. COMMON CONFUSIONS

| Confusion | Correct difference |
|---|---|
| "A letter is **wrote** by Ram" | Wrong — passive needs V3 (*written*), not V2 (*wrote*). |
| "A letter **has written** by Ram" | Wrong — *has written* is **active** perfect. Passive perfect is *has **been** written*. |
| "An apple **is eaten** by her" (for "She ate an apple") | Wrong tense — *is eaten* is present passive; the active was past, so the passive must be *was eaten*. |
| "A letter is ate by Ram" | Wrong twice — V3 *eaten* chahiye, aur past ke liye *was*. |
| Intransitive verbs (no object) | No object → no passive. "He sleeps" ka passive nahi banta — object hi nahi hai. |

### H. MEMORY TECHNIQUE (one only)

**"Passive = be + V3, hamesha."** Tense badlo to *be* badlo (is → was → has been → will be), lekin V3 hamesha wahi rahega. Bank question 002 ka jawab isi se milta hai: Present Simple passive = *is* + *written*.

## Chapter 3 — Direct & Indirect Narration

### A. VISUAL INTRODUCTION — the same words, reported later

```
DIRECT (as spoken):        He said,  "I am busy."
                                    ▲  quoted words — tense as spoken

INDIRECT (reported):       He said  that  he was busy.
                                    ▲  'that' joins · pronoun I → he · tense one step back (am → was)

SHIFT MAP (tense one step back):
  Present Simple  →  Past Simple        (am → was · is → was · write → wrote)
  Present Perfect →  Past Perfect       (has written → had written)
  Present Continuous → Past Continuous  (is reading → was reading)
  will            →  would              (will come → would come)
  can             →  could              (can do → could do)
```

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the indirect column and convert the direct sentences yourself. That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — "usne kaha" vs "usne kaha ki..."

Ram says, "**I am busy.**" You report it to Shyam later: "**He said that he was busy.**" Notice what changed: *I* became *he* (because you are talking about Ram now, not as Ram), *am* became *was* (because the reporting happened in the past — Ram *was* busy when he said it), and the inverted commas disappeared, replaced by *that*. The meaning is the same; the grammar moved one step into the past. That one-step-back shift is the whole chapter.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why the tense shifts back

- **Why does *am* become *was*?** Because the reporting verb (*said*) is in the past. The reported words were true at the moment of speaking, which is now in the past — so the tense follows the reporting verb backwards. (This is a convention of English grammar.)
- **Why does the pronoun change?** Because the speaker changes. *I* (Ram speaking) becomes *he* (you reporting about Ram). The pronoun follows who is speaking now.
- **Why does *that* appear?** It joins the reporting clause to the reported clause — it replaces the inverted commas. In everyday English it can be dropped ("He said he was busy"), but the exam's standard form keeps *that*.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — convert direct to indirect in 5 steps

1. **Reporting verb:** *said* stays *said*; if there is an object, *said to* becomes *told* ("He said to me" → "He told me").
2. **Add *that*** (for statements) and remove the inverted commas.
3. **Change the pronoun** — I → he/she, we → they, my → his/her, according to who is speaking.
4. **Shift the tense one step back** — Present Simple → Past Simple; Present Perfect → Past Perfect; *will* → *would*; *can* → *could*.
5. **Shift time/place words** — *today* → *that day*, *here* → *there*, *now* → *then*, *tomorrow* → *the next day*, *yesterday* → *the previous day*.

**Counter-example (where the rule does NOT apply):** a **universal truth** does not shift. Direct: "He said, 'The sun rises in the east.'" Indirect: "He said that the sun **rises** in the east." — *rises* stays present, because it is always true.

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture a **tape recorder played one day late**: the words are the same, but the "time stamp" on them has moved one step back — *am* has become *was*, *today* has become *that day*. The recording is identical; only the date on the label changed.

### F. IMPORTANT FACTS

| Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|
| "I **am** busy." | He said that he **was** busy. |
| "I **have finished**." | He said that he **had finished**. |
| "I **will come**." | He said that he **would come**. |
| "I **can** do it." | He said that he **could** do it. |
| "The sun **rises** in the east." (universal truth) | He said that the sun **rises** in the east. (no shift) |

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| *said* + object = *told* | "He **said to** me" → "He **told** me that…" |
| Questions | "He asked, 'Are you busy?'" → "He asked **if/whether** I was busy." (no question mark, no *that*) |
| Imperatives | "He said, 'Sit down.'" → "He **told** me **to sit** down." |
| Time words shift | today → that day · here → there · now → then · tomorrow → the next day · yesterday → the previous day |

### G. COMMON CONFUSIONS

| Confusion | Correct difference |
|---|---|
| "He said that he **is** busy" | Wrong — reporting verb *said* is past, so the tense must shift back to *was*. |
| "He said that he **has been** busy" | Wrong shift — *am* (Present Simple) goes to *was* (Past Simple), not to Present Perfect. |
| "He said that he **will be** busy" | Wrong direction — *will* would shift to *would*, and the original was present, not future. |
| *said* vs *told* | *Told* always needs an object ("He told **me**"); *said* does not ("He said that…"). |
| Universal truth shifting | "The sun rises in the east" stays *rises* in indirect speech — truths do not shift. |
| Question in indirect speech | No question mark, no *that*: "He asked **if** I was busy" — not "He asked that was I busy". |

### H. MEMORY TECHNIQUE (one only)

**"Ek kadam peeche"** — one step back. Present → Past, *will* → *would*, *can* → *could*, *today* → *that day*. The only exception: universal truths do not step back.

## Chapter 4 — Articles & Determiners

### A. VISUAL INTRODUCTION — the sound test

```
WORD STARTS WITH...        SOUND?           ARTICLE
  "hour"  (h silent)  →  'our' (vowel)   →  AN hour
  "university"          →  'yu' (consonant) → A university
  "apple"               →  'a' (vowel)   →  AN apple
  "book"                →  'b' (consonant) → A book

IS IT ONE SPECIFIC THING WE BOTH KNOW?
  Yes  →  THE  ("The book on the table" — kaun si kitab, pata hai)
  No   →  A / AN  ("A book" — koi ek kitab)

FLOW:  Specific? ──Yes──▶ THE
          │
          No
          │
       Vowel SOUND? ──Yes──▶ AN     ──No──▶ A
```

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the right column and decide the article for: *hour, university, umbrella, one-eyed man, honest man, European*. That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — "a university" and "an hour"

A student says, "I study in **an university**." It sounds careful — *u* is a vowel letter, so *an* feels right. It is wrong. Say the word aloud: **university** starts with a *yu* sound — a consonant sound — so it takes **a**: "a university". Now say **hour**: the *h* is silent, so the word begins with the vowel sound *our* — so it takes **an**: "an hour". The article never looks at the letter. It listens to the **sound**. And when both speakers already know *which* thing is meant — "the book on the table" — the article is **the**, because the thing is definite, identified, known.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why sound decides, not spelling

- **Why sound, not spelling?** Because *a/an* exists to make speech flow: *an* is used before a vowel sound so two vowel sounds do not collide ("a apple" is awkward; "an apple" flows). The rule follows the mouth, not the alphabet. (This is a convention of English pronunciation and usage.)
- **Why does *the* exist at all?** *A/an* means "one, any one" (indefinite — *ek*). *The* means "that one, the one we both know" (definite — *nishchit*). The speaker chooses between "any one" and "the known one" — that is the only job of the article.
- **Why is there no article sometimes?** General statements need none: "**Dogs** are loyal" (all dogs, in general) — no article. Add *the* and it becomes specific: "**The dogs** in my street are loyal."

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — choose the article in 3 questions

1. **Is it one specific, known thing?** → **The**. ("The sun rises in the east" — only one sun we mean.)
2. **If not specific: does the next word start with a vowel SOUND?** → **An**. ("an hour", "an honest man", "an umbrella", "an MBA" — *em* sound.)
3. **Otherwise → A.** ("a university", "a one-eyed man", "a European", "a book".)

**Determiners (beyond articles):** *this/that/these/those, some/any, much/many, each/every, all/both, my/your/his* are also determiners — they sit in the same place before a noun. Their detail is a **GAP box** in §F below (the old notes do not cover them; research is needed before teaching them as a chapter).

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture an **ear, not an eye**: the article is decided by what you *hear*, not what you *see* on the page. *Hour* looks like it starts with a consonant — but the ear hears a vowel. *University* looks like it starts with a vowel — but the ear hears *yu*. Listen first, then choose.

### G. COMMON CONFUSIONS

**Counter-example (where the "vowel letter = an" rule does NOT apply):** *university, uniform, European, one, useful* — all start with a vowel **letter** but a consonant **sound**, so they take **a**, not *an*.

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the labels and say which preposition fits: *___ Monday* (point) vs *___ three days* (duration). That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — "ill since Monday" and "ill for three days"

Ramesh has been ill. He fell ill **on Monday** — Monday is the day it started, a single point on the calendar. So: "He has been ill **since Monday**." His sister asks *how long* — three days, a stretch of time, a duration. So: "He has been ill **for three days**." Same illness, two questions: **when did it start?** (*since* + point) and **how long?** (*for* + duration). Mix them up and the sentence breaks: "ill since three days" is wrong, and so is "ill for Monday". The exam's favourite trap is exactly this swap.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why *since* and *for* are not interchangeable

- **Why *since* needs a point?** *Since* means "from this moment onwards" — it needs a starting moment (Monday, 2020, morning, 5 o'clock). A duration (three days) is not a moment, so *since* cannot take it. (A convention of English usage.)
- **Why *for* needs a duration?** *For* measures *how long* — it needs a length of time (three days, two years, a long time). A single day (Monday) is not a length, so *for* cannot take it.
- **Why are some prepositions fixed pairs (collocations)?** Because English has settled idioms: we say **crowded with** people, **good at** maths, **afraid of** dogs, **famous for** something — not "crowded of" or "good in". These pairs are conventions; they are learnt as pairs, not derived from a rule. The 2024 PYQ tested one: **crowded with** (2024-P2 Q15).

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — choose the preposition in 3 steps

1. **Time or place?** Time → *in/on/at* (see §F); place → *at* (point), *in* (inside/big place), *on* (surface).
2. **Point or duration?** Point (Monday, 2020, morning) → **since**. Duration (three days, two years) → **for**.
3. **Is it a fixed pair?** Check the collocation table in §F — *crowded with, good at, afraid of, interested in, famous for* — and use the pair as learnt.

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture a **calendar with a pin and a ruler**: *since* is the **pin** stuck into one day (Monday) — "from this pin onwards". *For* is the **ruler** measuring the stretch (three days) — "this long". Pin = since, ruler = for.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — the exam's favourite trick

The exam gives you **Susceptible** and asks for its **antonym**. The options are *Resistant, Vulnerable, Weak, Sensitive*. *Vulnerable* jumps out — it is a word you know, and it is close to *Susceptible* in meaning. That is exactly why it is there: it is the **synonym**, not the antonym. The correct answer is *Resistant* — the opposite. The exam does this on purpose: it puts the synonym in the options when it asks for the antonym, and the antonym in the options when it asks for the synonym. The first thing to read is not the word — it is the **question word**: *synonym* or *antonym*? Get that wrong and the "obvious" option is the trap. (This exact word was asked as 2024-P2 Q12.)

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why synonyms and antonyms are paired

- **Why does the exam pair them?** Because knowing a word means knowing both its friends and its enemies. If you know *Happy* only as "khush", you will pick *Sad* as its synonym by mistake — you need the pair (Happy–Joyful *and* Happy–Sad) to be safe. (This is a fact about how vocabulary is tested, not a law of nature.)
- **Why do some words have more than one synonym?** Because words overlap in meaning but are not identical: *Joyful* and *Glad* both mean "khush", but *Glad* is milder. The exam accepts the closest one — and the other options are usually antonyms or unrelated words, not near-synonyms.
- **Why is *Vulnerable* the trap for *Susceptible*?** Both mean "asani se prabhavit hone wala" — they are near-identical. The question asks for the *opposite*, so the near-identical word is the most dangerous option.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — answer any synonym/antonym question in 4 steps

1. **Read the question word first** — *synonym* (samaan) or *antonym* (vipreet)? Underline it mentally.
2. **Say the word's meaning in Hindi** — "Susceptible = asani se prabhavit hone wala."
3. **Sort the options into friends and enemies** — *Vulnerable* = friend (synonym), *Resistant* = enemy (antonym), *Weak/Sensitive* = neither.
4. **Pick from the right family** — antonym asked → enemy; synonym asked → friend.

## Chapter 7 — One-Word Substitution

### A. VISUAL INTRODUCTION — roots are building blocks

```
ROOT FAMILIES (paribhasha ka ek shabd = root ko pehchano):
  omni- = sab (all)          hydro- = pani (water)       -phobia = dar (fear)
    ├── omni-scient = sab janna      ├── hydro-phobia = pani se dar
    ├── omni-potent = sab kar sakna  └── hydro-electric = pani se bijli
    └── omni-present = har jagah hona
  ambi- = dono (both)        avi- = pakshi (bird)        api- = madhu (bee)
    └── ambi-dextrous = dono haath       ├── avi-ary = pakshiyon ki jagah
                                          └── api-ary = madhumakkhiyon ki jagah
  Narcissus (Greek katha) → Narcissist = khud se atyadhik prem karne wala
```

> **How to use this diagram:** Cover the meanings and say each root's meaning from the word; cover the words and build the word from the root. That is retrieval practice, not reading.

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — one word instead of a sentence

"A person who loves himself" is a whole sentence. The exam wants **one word** for it: **Narcissist**. The word comes from a Greek story — Narcissus, a young man who fell in love with his own reflection in the water and could not look away. So *Narcissist* means someone who admires himself excessively. Most one-word answers work the same way: a Latin or Greek root carries the meaning. *Omniscient* = *omni* (all) + *scient* (to know) = "one who knows everything". *Ambidextrous* = *ambi* (both) + *dexter* (right hand) = "one who can use both hands". *Hydrophobia* = *hydro* (water) + *phobia* (fear) = "fear of water". Learn the roots and the words build themselves.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why roots carry the meaning

- **Why do the roots work?** Because English borrowed thousands of words from Latin and Greek, and the roots kept their meanings. *Omni* always means "all", *hydro* always means "water", *phobia* always means "fear" — so a new word built from a known root can be decoded. (This is a fact about English word formation.)
- **Why is *Narcissist* not *Egoist*?** Both are about the self, but they are different: a *Narcissist* **admires himself excessively** (self-love, self-praise); an *Egoist* is **selfish** — he lives for his own benefit. The bank question was reworded to "admires himself excessively" precisely so that *Narcissist* is the unique answer (register §C2).
- **Why are *-ary* words about places?** The suffix *-ary* often marks a place for something: *aviary* (birds), *aquarium* (water animals), *apiary* (bees), *library* (books). The animal or thing before it tells you which place.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — find the one word in 4 steps

1. **Find the key word in the definition** — *knows* → *scient*; *hands* → *dexter*; *water* → *hydro*; *fear* → *phobia*; *place* → *-ary*.
2. **Match the root** — *omni* (all), *ambi* (both), *hydro* (water), *avi* (bird), *api* (bee), *aqua* (water).
3. **Build the word** — omni + scient = **Omniscient**; ambi + dextrous = **Ambidextrous**; hydro + phobia = **Hydrophobia**.
4. **Check the near-miss** — *Omnipotent* (can do everything) is not *Omniscient* (knows everything); *Dexterous* (skilful) is not *Ambidextrous* (both hands).

### E. VISUAL MEMORY CONNECTION

Picture **Lego bricks**: each root is a brick with its meaning written on it — *omni* (sab), *hydro* (pani), *phobia* (dar), *avi* (pakshi). Snap two bricks together and the word appears: *omni* + *scient* = "sab janne wala". The exam gives you the finished toy and asks which bricks it is made of.

### F. IMPORTANT FACTS

| Definition | One word | Root break-down |

### B. STORY-BASED EXPLANATION — the camel and the ship

The bank gives a short passage: *"The camel is called the ship of the desert. It can walk for many days in the hot desert without water. It carries people and heavy loads across the sand."* The question: why is the camel called the "ship of the desert"? One option says "It can live without water for many days" — that is **true** (the passage says so), but it is **not the answer**: a ship is not defined by going without water, it is defined by **carrying** people and loads across a vast expanse. The correct option is "It carries people and loads across the desert" — because the *comparison* is about carrying, not about thirst. This is the whole chapter in one question: a true statement is not the same as the answer to the question asked.

### C. WHY DOES IT HAPPEN? — why passages trap you

- **Why read the questions first?** Because then you read with a purpose — you know what to look for (a reason, a comparison, a fact), instead of trying to remember the whole passage. Reading without a question is memorisation; reading with a question is searching. (This is a standard reading strategy.)
- **Why do options copy passage words?** To trap the reader who matches words instead of meaning. An option that repeats the passage's words ("without water for many days") *feels* right — but if it does not answer the question asked, it is wrong. **Exact wording ≠ correct answer.**
- **Why the first and last paragraphs?** Because a passage usually states its main idea at the start and restates it at the end. The middle is evidence and detail. If you only have time to read two paragraphs carefully, read those two.

### D. HOW DOES IT WORK? — solve any passage in 5 steps

1. **Read the questions first** — know what is being asked before you read.
2. **Skim the first and last paragraphs** — find the main idea.
3. **Read the whole passage** — now read for the answers, underlining the lines that match each question.
4. **Answer from the passage, not from general knowledge** — if the passage does not say it, do not assume it. (The camel "lives only in Rajasthan" is wrong — the passage does not say it, and it is false.)
5. **Check every option against the question, not against the passage** — an option can be true *and* wrong, if it answers a different question (bank question 020, option B).
